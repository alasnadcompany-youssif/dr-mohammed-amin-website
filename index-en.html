Clinic Services

Essential cardiac tests that help evaluate heart function, follow heart rhythm, and assess exercise response according to each patient's needs.

ECG

Electrocardiogram

A quick test that records the heart's electrical activity and helps assess rhythm, palpitations, and early cardiac changes.

Echo

Echocardiography

An ultrasound test that evaluates heart muscle, valves, pumping strength, and wall motion in a non-invasive way.

Stress

Stress Test

A test that evaluates how the heart responds to effort and whether symptoms appear during physical activity.

Holter

Holter Monitoring

A portable monitor that records heart rhythm over a longer period to detect palpitations or rhythm changes missed by a routine ECG.

Subspecialty

Cardiac Electrophysiology and Rhythm Disorders

Cardiac electrophysiology focuses on the heart's electrical system and rhythm disorders such as palpitations, sudden tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, slow heart rhythm, or irregular heartbeats.

Each patient is assessed clinically, then the appropriate tests are selected, such as ECG, Holter monitoring, echocardiography, or an electrophysiology study when needed.

Instructions & Guidance

Essential Guidance for Tests, Follow-Up, and Urgent Warning Signs

1 / 6
Before Visit

Prepare your medical information

Bring your medication list, previous tests, catheterization reports or ECGs if available, and recent blood pressure or pulse readings.

Holter

Holter monitor instructions

The Holter records rhythm during the day. Avoid wetting or removing electrodes, and note the timing of palpitations or dizziness.

Stress Test

Prepare properly for the test

Wear comfortable walking clothes and shoes, and do not stop any medication before the test unless the doctor asks you to.

After Cath

Watch the access site

Rest as instructed and seek care if there is bleeding, marked swelling, increasing pain, fever, or concerning symptoms.

Medications

Do not stop treatment yourself

Blood pressure medicine, blood thinners, and rhythm medications require careful adherence. Any change should be discussed with the doctor.

Urgent Alert

When to seek emergency care

Severe chest pain, acute shortness of breath, fainting, sweating with chest pain, or palpitations with severe dizziness need urgent assessment.

FAQ

Chest Pain

If the pain is severe, new, or comes with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, dizziness, or spreads to the shoulder, arm, or jaw, it should be treated as an emergency.

Not always. Chest pain may come from the heart, stomach, muscles, or lungs, but important cardiac causes are usually ruled out first because they can be serious.

Depending on the case, the patient may need ECG, cardiac enzyme blood tests, echocardiography, stress testing, or catheterization if results indicate it.

Palpitations

Medical assessment is needed if palpitations are recurrent, prolonged, worsening, or associated with chest pain, fainting, severe dizziness, or shortness of breath.

No. Some cases are related to anxiety, stimulants, poor sleep, or stress, but recurrent palpitations may indicate a heart rhythm disorder.

Common tests include ECG, Holter monitoring, echocardiography, blood tests, and sometimes an electrophysiology study depending on the doctor's assessment.

Heart Rhythm Disorders

It means the heartbeat may be too fast, too slow, or irregular, and it may appear as palpitations, dizziness, fatigue, or shortness of breath.

If it is associated with chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or fainting, emergency care is needed because some rhythm disorders require urgent treatment.

Yes. Treatment may include medication, lifestyle changes, cardioversion, catheter ablation, or implanted devices depending on the type and severity.

Atrial Fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation is a rhythm disorder where the pulse becomes irregular. Patients may feel palpitations, fatigue, shortness of breath, or dizziness.

It may increase stroke risk if not followed properly. The doctor decides whether blood thinners or rhythm/rate control medications are needed.

Treatment may include rate-control medication, blood thinners, rhythm-control medication, cardioversion, or catheter ablation in selected cases.

High Blood Pressure

Often it causes no clear symptoms, which is why regular measurement is important instead of relying only on how a person feels.

The decision depends on repeated readings, age, diabetes, kidney or heart disease, and overall cardiovascular risk. Treatment may include lifestyle changes and medications.

No. Do not stop medication on your own. If readings improve, the doctor decides whether to reduce or adjust treatment.

Diagnostic Cardiac Catheterization

It may be requested for chest pain, abnormal stress test or ECG findings, suspected coronary narrowing, or the need for accurate assessment of coronary arteries.

The medical team may ask for fasting, a full medication list, especially blood thinners and diabetes medications, and information about allergies, kidney disease, or pregnancy.

It is usually done with local anesthesia and mild sedation. The patient may feel pressure or mild discomfort at the access site, but severe pain is not typical.

After Catheterization or Stent

Rest, drink fluids if allowed, monitor the access site, keep the area clean and dry, and avoid exertion according to the doctor's instructions.

This depends on the procedure type and access site. Heavy lifting and strenuous activity are usually avoided for a short period, and return to work depends on the case and job type.

No. Stopping blood thinners or antiplatelet medication without medical advice may cause stent blockage or a clot. Take medication exactly as prescribed.

Cardiac Electrophysiology

It is specialized testing and assessment of the heart's electrical system to identify the source of rhythm disorders, especially with recurrent palpitations or sudden tachycardia.

It may be needed for recurrent palpitations, sudden tachycardia, unexplained fainting, or rhythm disorders not clearly captured by routine tests.

Some rhythm disorders can be treated with catheter ablation, a procedure used to stop abnormal electrical signals in the heart.

Echocardiography

An echo is an ultrasound of the heart that shows heart movement, pumping strength, valves, and blood flow inside the heart.

It may be requested for shortness of breath, chest pain, heart murmur, weak heart muscle, valve disease, or follow-up of a known cardiac condition.

No. It is usually painless and uses sound waves to create images of the heart.

Stress Test

It is used to evaluate the heart during exercise and may help diagnose coronary artery problems or some rhythm disorders.

No. The doctor decides based on age, symptoms, ECG, blood pressure, and walking ability. Some patients need alternative tests.

Do not stop any medication unless the doctor asks you to, because some medications may affect the test result or patient safety.
